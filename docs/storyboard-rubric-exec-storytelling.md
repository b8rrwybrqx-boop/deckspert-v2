This is the scoring standard behind the Storyboard check in Deckspert. It
follows the EPS Planning Worksheet exactly: the same eight boxes, in the same
order, with the same names attendees see. Each section's "What it must do" is
the worksheet prompt, and the anchors test for what that prompt asks for, plus
what EPS teaches for that box. It is the companion to the Proper Prep Scoring
Rubric: Proper Prep decides the audience, the recommendation and the pushback.
The Storyboard turns them into a story.

## How the scale works

Every section is scored 1 to 5 on quality, and the score sets the status the
attendee sees:

| Score | Status | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 5-4 | **PRESENT** | Clearly there and doing its job. |
| 3-2 | **WEAK** | Present but underdeveloped, vague, generic, or off-target. |
| 1 | **MISSING** | Genuinely absent from the storyboard. |

The distinction that matters: a section that is present but rough is weak, never
missing. A storyboard is a draft by nature, so most sections should land at 2 or
3 on the first pass and climb on the re-run. If nearly everything scores 1 on a
first attempt, the rubric is miscalibrated, not the cohort.

**Inform vs Persuasive.** The worksheet frames the story as Inform ("Here's the
single conclusion I want you to leave with") or Persuasive ("Here's what I'm
asking you to say yes to"). For 80/20 Champions the default is Inform, and the
ask is adoption rather than approval: the yes isn't "start this project," it's
"don't stop here." The anchors assume Inform and note where Persuasive differs.

## 1) INTRODUCTION: Hook Them & Ground Them

### 1a) Opening Gambit

**What it must do.** Two jobs, in this order. Hook the room and grab attention.
Then share the key headline of why they are there. Inform: the single conclusion
you want them to leave with. Persuasive: what you're asking them to say yes to.

| | |
|---|---|
| **5 STRONG** | Both jobs done in two or three sentences. The hook starts in the audience's world with something true and surprising (a question, fact, anecdote, quotation or analogy). The headline follows immediately. Passes the Swap Test. |
| **3 WEAK** | One job done, the other missing or weak: a hook with no headline, or a headline buried under setup. Or it would work just as well for someone else's project. |
| **1 MISSING** | No hook and no headline. Opens with the agenda, credentials or background. |

**Benchmark (Trilogy):** "You would expect your best customer to be one of your
easiest customers to serve. Motorola represented 18% of Trilogy's revenue, but
70% of its transactions." Then the headline: "Motorola was not the problem.
Unmanaged complexity was."

**Most common failure:** Starting with "Today I'm going to walk you through..."
That is a topic label, not a story.

**Quick test:** The Swap Test. Could this exact opening start someone else's
presentation? If yes, it is generic: score no higher than 3.

### 1b) What is the Situation & Root Cause Why

**What it must do.** Ground the audience in the data that defined the project:
what the numbers were, what the root cause was, and why this was the most
important problem to solve.

| | |
|---|---|
| **5 STRONG** | All three are there: the numbers that defined the problem, one real root cause (why it happened, not just what happened), and why this problem mattered more than others. |
| **3 WEAK** | Numbers without a root cause, or a "root cause" that restates a symptom ("too many manual steps" rather than why the steps exist). Or a data dump with no interpretation. |
| **1 MISSING** | Background only. No numbers and no root cause. |

**Benchmark (Trilogy):** "Exceptions had become the service model. Each special
request became precedent for the next. With no guardrails, complexity compounded
quietly until it was the ceiling on our growth."

**Most common failure:** Stopping at the what. Anyone can show a gap; the root
cause is what separates information from insight.

## 2) EXPLANATION: Deliver the Pivot and the Proof

### 2a) So What is the Big Idea

**What it must do.** The single insight that drove the approach, or that the
team discovered along the way. What did they have to believe that made the
difference? A belief, not an action, and the mirror of the root cause. What the
room should remember when they walk out.

| | |
|---|---|
| **5 STRONG** | A memorable line, phrased as a belief, that visibly flips the root cause in 1b, often followed by one sentence on what it means for the business. The bridge sentence reads cleanly. |
| **3 WEAK** | Present but written as an initiative or action plan, or so broad nobody could disagree. Or several ideas competing for the box. |
| **1 MISSING** | Absent, or indistinguishable from the list of actions in 2b. |

**Benchmark (Trilogy):** "Complexity is a tax on growth. To serve Motorola better
and create capacity for new business, Trilogy has to stop absorbing exceptions
and start eliminating them." The first line flips the root cause (complexity had
become the ceiling on growth) and is the one the room remembers. The second
follows the "To [benefit], you need to [belief]" structure and bridges into the
actions.

**Most common failure:** Writing the big initiative instead of the big idea. If
it starts with a verb you could drop into a project plan, it belongs in 2b.

**Quick test:** The bridge sentence. "Because [root cause from 1b], we believe
[Big Idea], so we [the steps in 2b]." If it doesn't read cleanly, one of the
three boxes is weak. Name which one.

### 2b) Now What You Did

**What it must do.** The approach in concrete steps. Not everything you did,
just what mattered most. Easy to follow, easy to replicate.

| | |
|---|---|
| **5 STRONG** | Two to four sequenced steps, each visibly attacking the root cause. A future team could replicate the approach from this box alone. |
| **3 WEAK** | Steps are present but either exhaustive (every task performed) or vague (verbs with no object). The link back to the root cause is implied rather than shown. |
| **1 MISSING** | An activity list with no structure, or absent. |

**Benchmark (Trilogy):** A reframe ("We stopped asking 'How do we manage all of
this work?' and started asking 'Which of this work should exist at all?'"),
then three steps: expose the complexity, eliminate low-value activity,
standardize the work with red rule / green rule guardrails. Each ties straight
back to the exceptions problem.

**Most common failure:** Showing the depth of the analysis rather than the shape
of the approach. The detail belongs in the appendix.

### 2c) WIIFM

**What it must do.** The impact. What changed as a result of the project? Lead
with the financial metric, then add on-time delivery, capacity or simplification
results where relevant.

| | |
|---|---|
| **5 STRONG** | Leads with a hard financial number, then two or three supporting results (on-time delivery, capacity, simplification). Results that happened, stated specifically. |
| **3 WEAK** | Results are real but vague ("improved efficiency," "strong ROI"), there is no financial number, or it restates the activities in 2b instead of their impact. |
| **1 MISSING** | Absent, or no measurable change named. |

**Benchmark (Trilogy):** "$200K in annual savings", then improved on-time
delivery, less overhead, more capacity to scale, a repeatable playbook.

**Most common failure:** "This improved efficiency." Without a number, senior
leaders can't tell how much it mattered.

## 3) CONCLUSION: Land the Lesson & Look Ahead

### 3a) Your Recommendation / Executive Takeaway

**What it must do.** Based on what the team learned, what should the
organization do next? The single conclusion senior leaders should leave the room
with, and which of the six recommendation types it is: sustain, scale,
replicate, adjust, avoid or monitor. Persuasive: clearly restate what you want
them to say yes to.

| | |
|---|---|
| **5 STRONG** | One clear, unhedged sentence, stated as a direction. Obvious which of the six types it is. It follows from the Big Idea, says where to apply it next, and matches the headline in 1a. |
| **3 WEAK** | A recommendation exists but the type is unclear, it hedges ("we could consider…"), it offers a menu of options instead of one direction, or it doesn't match the 1a headline. |
| **1 MISSING** | No recommendation. The story ends with results and gives leaders no direction. |

**Benchmark (Trilogy):** "Apply the same complexity audit to the next customer
or product segment showing high transaction volume, excessive exceptions,
disproportionate overhead, and service that has become 'just the way we do
it'." That is Scale: extending what worked to other customers and products.

**Most common failure:** Not deciding which type it is before writing a single
slide. Close behind: offering options and letting the room choose. Executives
want a recommendation, not a menu.

**Calibration note:** Advanced Champions will almost always land on Scale or
Replicate, and the line between them is thin. Require a team to name one. Don't
mark them down for choosing its neighbor.

### 3b) Summarize & Gain Commitment | Close

**What it must do.** Come back to the Big Idea. Restate what changed and why it
matters. Name the tradeoff: what it costs not to act. End strong. Don't trail
off.

| | |
|---|---|
| **5 STRONG** | Reconnects explicitly to the Big Idea, restates what changed with the headline number, names what it costs not to act, and ends on a strong final line. Persuasive: ends on the decision being asked for. |
| **3 WEAK** | Summarizes accurately but re-presents the whole case, never reconnects to the Big Idea, skips the cost of not acting, or ends on a thank-you. |
| **1 MISSING** | Trails off, or absent. |

**Benchmark (Trilogy):** "Motorola was not the problem. Unmanaged complexity was
the problem. When we applied 80/20 to how we served the customer, not just
which customers we served, we released $200K in savings and created a more
scalable operating model." Strong on the Big Idea and the number, but no
tradeoff: a 3 as written. Adding the tradeoff from Module 2 ("Complexity
returns. The wins don't scale.") makes it a 5.

**Most common failure:** Re-presenting the case instead of landing it. The close
is a few sentences, not a second presentation.

### 3c) Actions & Next Steps

**What it must do.** What specific actions should the room leave with? Name
them. Own them. Give them a timeline.

| | |
|---|---|
| **5 STRONG** | Three to five concrete next steps, each with an owner and a timeline, that follow from the recommendation rather than repeating 2b. The ask of the room is named, not implied. |
| **3 WEAK** | Actions are listed but carry no owners or dates, or they restate work already completed. |
| **1 MISSING** | Absent, or a vague intention to continue. |

**Benchmark (Trilogy):** Select the next segment for review; formalize the red
rule / green rule framework; document the Trilogy playbook; share the case with
the next cohort of 80/20 Champions. Strong actions, but with no owners or dates,
this scores a 3 as written. A name and a date on each makes it a 5. Executives
say it themselves: "Tell me who's accountable and by when."

**Most common failure:** Listing what happened instead of what happens next.

## Across the whole storyboard: the connections

Alongside the eight boxes, the tool checks that the boxes connect. These are not
scored, but they are where the most useful coaching lands.

- **Headline and takeaway.** The 1a headline and the 3a recommendation say the
  same thing. If they don't, the story changed direction in the middle.
- **Root cause and Big Idea.** The 2a Big Idea visibly flips the 1b root cause.
  The bridge sentence is the test.
- **Close and Big Idea.** 3b comes back to the Big Idea in the same words, not a
  new idea.
- **Actions and recommendation.** 3c follows from 3a: next steps, not a replay
  of 2b.
- **The adoption sequence.** Module 2's four steps run through the conclusion:
  the Recommendation (3a, stated upfront in 1a), the rationale (2c WIIFM), the
  tradeoff (3b) and the ask (3c). If one is missing, the ask won't land.
- **Discipline.** One beat each for the Opening Gambit, Situation and Big Idea.
  Two to four steps in Now What You Did. WIIFM and the Close kept tight.
  Anything else belongs in an appendix.

## Decisions built into this version

1. **Recommendation types.** All six, as taught: sustain, scale, replicate,
   adjust, avoid, monitor. The worksheet prompt lists four; the rubric accepts
   all six. Same rule as the Proper Prep rubric.
2. **The Trilogy type.** Scale, in both rubrics.
3. **The tradeoff.** Credited in the Close for every 80/20 story, not only
   Persuasive ones. Recommended: add "and what it costs not to act" to the Close
   prompt on the worksheet, so attendees are asked for what they are scored on.
4. **Owners and dates.** Required for a 5 on Actions & Next Steps. The Trilogy
   example scores a 3 on this box, and on the Close, until it adds names, dates
   and the tradeoff line.
5. **The Trilogy Big Idea.** The full two-sentence version is the benchmark. The
   Bridge Test slide and the Flip It slide use its second half, which is
   consistent: no change needed.
