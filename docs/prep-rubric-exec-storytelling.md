# Proper Prep Scoring Rubric

**Deckspert · Executive Presentation Skills · Marmon 80/20 Champions**

> Final, from Todd Bradley (2026-09-30). Implemented in
> `core/programs/marmon8020.ts` (`prep`). The general Deckspert version, for the
> full Proper Prep worksheet, is `prep-rubric-deckspert-draft.md`.

This is the scoring standard behind the Proper Prep check in Deckspert for
Executive Presentation Skills. It follows the EPS Planning Worksheet | Proper
Preparation – 80/20 Project box for box, using the worksheet's own names, with
the Trilogy example worksheet as the benchmark. It is the companion to the
Storyboard Scoring Rubric: Proper Prep is where the audience, the recommendation
and the pushback are decided. The Storyboard is where they become a story.

## How the scale works

The worksheet has two kinds of box.

**Checked, not scored (yes / no).** Audience & Title/Role and Behavioral Style
are filled in or they are not. They show as PRESENT or MISSING, with no 1 to 5
score, and never affect the quality scores.

**Scored 1 to 5 on quality.** The other five boxes. The score sets the status:

| Score | Status | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 5-4 | **PRESENT** | Clearly there and doing its job. |
| 3-2 | **WEAK** | Present but underdeveloped, vague, generic, or off-target. |
| 1 | **MISSING** | Genuinely absent from the worksheet. |

A box that is present but rough is weak, never missing. Prep is pre-work, so
most boxes should land at 2 or 3 on the first pass and climb after the Module 1
refinement. If nearly everything scores 1 on a first attempt, the rubric is
miscalibrated, not the cohort. Use 5, 3 and 1 as anchors: 4 when a box nearly
meets the 5 anchor, 2 when it is present but clearly short of the 3 anchor.

**Brevity is not weakness.** The worksheet is short phrases in table cells, and
the Trilogy example is written that way. Judge specificity, not length.

**Calibration standard.** The Trilogy example worksheet is the known-good
worksheet. Run through the evaluator, it should read "strong," with every scored
box at 4 or 5. If it doesn't, the evaluator is off, not the example.

**Inform vs Persuasive.** For 80/20 Champions the default is Inform: the "yes"
being sought is adoption of what the project proved, not approval of a new plan.
As Module 2 puts it, the yes isn't "start this project," it's "don't stop here."
For a Persuasive story, the Recommendation must clearly restate what the
audience is being asked to say yes to.

## 1) THE AUDIENCE: Who Is in the Room

### 1a) Audience/Who & Title/Role *(checked, not scored)*

**What it must do.** Say who the audience is and their title or role.

| | |
|---|---|
| **PRESENT** | Both are filled in. |
| **MISSING** | Either is blank. |

**Benchmark (Trilogy):** "Project Sponsor" / "Key Decision Maker, Most
Influence." Present.

### 1b) Behavioral Style *(checked, not scored)*

**What it must do.** Tick one of Thinker, Director, Socializer or Relater.

| | |
|---|---|
| **PRESENT** | One style is ticked. |
| **MISSING** | None ticked. |

Feedback names the style and its move, straight from the Know Their Style slide.
**Director** ("Show me the bottom line"): lead with the answer, be brief, skip
the backstory. **Thinker** ("Show me the evidence"): lead with data, anticipate
questions, be rigorous. **Socializer** ("Show me the vision"): lead with the
story, connect to what excites them. **Relater** ("Show concern for me and my
team"): lead with the impact on people, acknowledge the team.

**Benchmark (Trilogy):** Director. Present, so the Recommendation should lead
with the bottom line, briefly, which it does.

## 2) THE NEEDS: What They Need From This

### 2a) Business Needs (Help Me Win)

**What it must do.** The specific business needs this audience has, tied to
their strategic priorities, not the project's. Each is marked Y or N for whether
it is addressed.

| | |
|---|---|
| **5 STRONG** | Two to four needs, each specific to this audience and connected to their strategic priorities: the return, the risk, the result they are accountable for. Each is marked Y, and the Y is believable: the Recommendation and WIIFM actually address it. |
| **3 WEAK** | Needs are real but generic ("grow the business", "cut costs"), or they are the presenter's needs rather than the audience's, or the Y/N column is blank or marked Y where nothing below addresses it. |
| **1 MISSING** | Absent. |

**Benchmark (Trilogy):** Proof the 80/20 investment is generating real ROI; a
repeatable approach, not a one-off win; freed capacity to serve and grow the 80
customers; operational efficiency (OTD, overhead, margin). All Y, and each one is
answered by the $200K, the "proven and ready to scale" framework, or the OTD
results.

**Most common failure:** Listing what the project needs from the room (sign-off,
resources) instead of what the room needs.

**Carries forward:** The needs marked Y are what the Storyboard's WIIFM has to
answer.

### 2b) Personal Needs (Know Me)

**What it must do.** What the decision-maker needs personally, each marked Y or
N for whether it is addressed.

| | |
|---|---|
| **5 STRONG** | One or two needs that are personal to this executive: what they are measured on, what they have staked their name on, what they want to be true. Marked Y, and believably addressed. |
| **3 WEAK** | Business needs restated in personal language ("they want the company to grow"), or a generic trait ("wants results"), or the Y/N column blank. |
| **1 MISSING** | Absent. |

**Benchmark (Trilogy):** Validation that sponsoring this program was the right
call; confidence the Champions are developing into stronger leaders. Both are
about the sponsor, not the business.

**Most common failure:** The mirror problem. If you swap "they" for "the
company" and the sentence still works, it is a business need.

**Also common:** Copying the Know Me list. "Don't waste my time" and "No
surprises" are true of every executive and say nothing about this one: score
them as generic. Use the list as a prompt instead. What would make this person
look smart to their boss?

**Quick test:** What would they be praised or blamed for? If the box does not
answer that, it has not reached personal. Coaching only, not a scoring cap.

## 3) THE ANSWER: What You Want Them to Leave With

### 3a) YOUR Recommendation / Executive Takeaway

**What it must do.** Based on what the team learned, what should the
organization do next? The single conclusion senior leaders should leave the room
with, and which of the six recommendation types it is: sustain, scale,
replicate, adjust, avoid or monitor.

| | |
|---|---|
| **5 STRONG** | One or two unhedged sentences. It is obvious which of the six types it is, and it says where to apply it next. It answers the needs marked Y above. Persuasive: it clearly restates what the audience is being asked to say yes to. |
| **3 WEAK** | A recommendation exists but the type is unclear, it hedges ("we could consider…"), it offers a menu of options instead of one direction, or it summarizes results rather than giving a direction. |
| **1 MISSING** | Absent, or the box describes what the team did. |

**Benchmark (Trilogy):** "Apply the same complexity audit to the next customer
or product segment before it reaches the same breaking point. The framework is
proven and ready to scale." That is Scale: extending what worked to other
customers and products.

**Most common failure:** Not deciding which type it is before writing. Close
behind: offering options and letting the room choose. Executives want a
recommendation, not a menu.

**Calibration note:** Advanced Champions will almost always land on Scale or
Replicate, and the line between them is thin. Require a team to name one. Don't
mark them down for choosing its neighbor.

**Carries forward:** This becomes the Storyboard's Opening Gambit headline and
its Recommendation / Executive Takeaway box, and the two should say the same
thing. Getting it right here is what prevents a buried recommendation later.

### 3b) WHY IT MATTERS / WIIFM: How the Organization Benefits

**What it must do.** How the organization benefits: the result, leading with
the number, and why it matters beyond this one project.

| | |
|---|---|
| **5 STRONG** | Leads with a hard result (ideally financial), then says why it matters beyond this one project. Specific, and in terms this audience cares about. |
| **3 WEAK** | The benefit is real but vague ("improved efficiency", "strong ROI"), there is no number, or it restates the activities instead of their impact. |
| **1 MISSING** | Absent, or no benefit named. |

**Benchmark (Trilogy):** "One project. $200K. If the same lens is applied across
other relationships with similar complexity profiles, the cumulative impact is
significant, and the next team doesn't have to start from scratch."

**Most common failure:** "This improved efficiency." Without a number, senior
leaders cannot tell how much it mattered.

**Stronger still:** It also says what it costs not to act ("Complexity returns.
The wins don't scale."). That line becomes the tradeoff in the Storyboard's
Close.

### 3c) QUESTIONS or Pushback You May Face

**What it must do.** The hard questions this room will actually ask, in the
room's own words.

| | |
|---|---|
| **5 STRONG** | Three or more specific questions, worded the way this executive would ask them, including at least one the presenter would rather not be asked. |
| **3 WEAK** | Generic worries ("they may ask about cost") rather than questions, or only easy questions the presenter is comfortable answering. |
| **1 MISSING** | Absent. |

**Benchmark (Trilogy):** "Are these savings real and sustainable, or will
Motorola push back?" "What's the risk of damaging the relationship?" "How do we
identify which segment to go after next?" "Is the timeline realistic for other
teams?" The first two are the uncomfortable ones.

**Most common failure:** Writing topics instead of questions. "ROI" is a topic;
"Are these savings real?" is the question they will ask.

**Why it matters:** This is where "pressure breaks structure" is prevented. A
question already written down is far less likely to pull the presenter off
message.

**Carries forward:** These become the challenge questions in the Module 4
Interruption Drill, so the pressure in practice is the pressure they will
actually face.

## Across the whole worksheet: the connections

Not scored, but where the most useful coaching lands.

- **Needs and answer.** Every need marked Y is addressed by the Recommendation
  or the WIIFM. A Y with nothing behind it is flagged. (The worksheet's column
  says "Addressed by Desired Outcome"; on this sheet the Recommendation is the
  desired outcome.)
- **Recommendation and WIIFM.** The WIIFM is the benefit of doing what the
  Recommendation says, not a separate list of results.
- **Recommendation and pushback.** The questions include the ones the
  Recommendation will provoke, not only questions about the past project.
- **Style and approach.** The ticked style shows up in the answer. A Director
  gets a bottom-line Recommendation with no backstory. A Thinker gets evidence
  in the WIIFM and the most thorough Questions or Pushback box, because
  anticipating questions is the Thinker move. A Socializer gets the story and
  the bigger picture. A Relater gets the impact on people and the team.

## Overall read

| Read | When |
|---|---|
| **needs work** | 3 or more scored boxes score 1, OR the Recommendation is missing |
| **mixed** | Some real strengths, but 1 to 2 critical gaps or broken connections |
| **strong** | No scored box below 3, the Recommendation and WIIFM both score 4 or higher, and needs marked Y are actually addressed |

## Decisions built into this version

1. **Recommendation types.** All six, as taught: sustain, scale, replicate,
   adjust, avoid, monitor. The worksheet prompt lists four; the rubric accepts
   all six. Same rule as the Storyboard rubric.
2. **The Trilogy type.** Scale, in both rubrics. It extends the audit to other
   customers and product segments, which is the Scale definition, and the
   example's own wording ("ready to scale") agrees.
3. **Overlap with the Storyboard.** Recommendation and WIIFM are scored in both,
   on purpose. Proper Prep judges the direction and the number. The Storyboard
   judges how they land in the story.
4. **Y/N column.** Coaching only, never a deduction. A need marked N prompts
   "address it or drop it." A Y with nothing behind it is flagged under the
   connections.
